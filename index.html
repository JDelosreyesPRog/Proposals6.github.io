Article 1156

Concept of Obligation

Core Legal Concept

An obligation is a juridical necessity to give, to do, or not to do (Civil Code of the Philippines). It creates a binding legal tie (vinculum juris) where the creditor can compel the debtor to perform an act or prestation in court.

Essential Elements Breakdown
  • Active Subject (Creditor / Obligee): The person entitled to demand performance.
  • Passive Subject (Debtor / Obligor): The person bound to fulfill the obligation.
  • Prestation (Object): The required conduct (to give, to do, or not to do).
  • Juridical Tie (Vinculum Juris): The legal relation connecting the parties created by law or contract.
💡 Everyday Real-Life Scenario (Halimbawa)

Buying a Smartphone Online: When you purchase a smartphone from a merchant for ₱15,000, you are the debtor obligated to pay the money, and the merchant is the creditor. Once paid, the roles flip: the merchant becomes the debtor obligated to deliver the phone (prestation), and you become the creditor entitled to demand delivery.

Article 1157

Sources of Obligations

Core Legal Concept

Obligations arise exclusively from five distinct legal sources under Philippine civil law:

  • 1. Law (Ex Lege): Expressly established by statute (e.g., duty to pay taxes under the Tax Code).
  • 2. Contracts (Ex Contractu): Voluntary agreements meeting all legal requisites (Art. 1305).
  • 3. Quasi-Contracts (Ex Quasi-Contractu): Lawful, voluntary, and unilateral acts to prevent unjust enrichment (e.g., Solutio Indebiti - erroneous payment).
  • 4. Acts/Omissions Punished by Law (Delicts): Civil liability arising from commission of a crime (Art. 100, Revised Penal Code).
  • 5. Quasi-Delicts / Torts (Ex Quasi-Delicto): Damage caused by fault or negligence where no pre-existing contract exists (Art. 2176).
💡 Real-Life Scenario: Quasi-Delict vs. Quasi-Contract

Quasi-Delict: A reckless driver crashes into your parked motorcycle. There is no contract between you, but the driver is legally obligated to pay for damages due to negligence.

Quasi-Contract: If GCash accidentally transfers ₱2,000 into your wallet due to a system glitch, you are legally bound to return it under Solutio Indebiti.

Articles 1163 - 1174

Nature and Effect of Obligations

Core Legal Concept & Standard of Care

Every person obliged to give something is bound to take care of it with the diligence of a good father of a family (bonus paterfamilias), unless the law or stipulation requires another standard of care.

Grounds for Damages (Art. 1170)
  • Fraud (Dolo): Deliberate intent to evade normal fulfillment of an obligation.
  • Negligence (Culpa): Omission of required diligence without deliberate bad intent.
  • Delay (Mora): Failure to perform on time after judicial or extrajudicial demand (Mora Solvendi = debtor delay; Mora Accipiendi = creditor delay).
  • Contravention of Tenor: Direct violation of any explicit terms of the obligation.
Fortuitous Events / Caso Fortuito (Art. 1174)

No person shall be held liable for events that could not be foreseen, or which, though foreseen, were inevitable (acts of God or force majeure), EXCEPT when specified by law or contract, or when nature of obligation requires assumption of risk.

💡 Real-Life Scenario: Fortuitous Event Exception

A car rental agency is supposed to deliver a van on Friday. If an unexpected super-typhoon causes flash floods that destroy the van before Friday, they are excused due to a Fortuitous Event. HOWEVER, if the agency was ALREADY in delay (Mora) before the typhoon struck, they remain liable for damages!

Articles 1305 & 1318

Definition & Essential Requisites of Contracts

Definition (Art. 1305)

A contract is a meeting of minds between two persons whereby one binds himself, with respect to the other, to give something or to render some service.

Three Essential Requisites (Art. 1318)

There is NO contract unless the following three elements concur:

  • 1. Consent: Manifested by the meeting of the offer and the acceptance upon the thing and the cause. Must be intelligent, free, and voluntary.
  • 2. Object Certain: The subject matter of the contract. Must be licit, possible, and determinate or determinable.
  • 3. Cause / Consideration: The essential reason why each party enters into the agreement (e.g., price for seller, item for buyer).
💡 Real-Life Scenario: Freelance IT Contract

An IT student agrees to build a website for a local bakery for ₱20,000. Consent: Both signed the agreement freely. Object: Web development services. Cause: ₱20,000 payment for the student, and completed website for the client. All 3 requisites are met, creating a binding contract.

Articles 1380 & 1409

Defective Contracts Classification

Under Philippine law, defective contracts are categorized into 4 levels according to severity of defect:

1. Rescissible

Valid until rescinded. Defect is economic damage or financial lesion to a party or third person (e.g., sale of property to defraud creditors).

2. Voidable

Valid until annulled. Defect is vitiated consent (intimidation, fraud, mistake) or incapacity of one party (e.g., contract signed by a minor).

3. Unenforceable

Cannot be enforced in court unless ratified. Defect is lack of authority, failure to comply with Statute of Frauds, or both parties incapable.

4. Void / Inexistent

Produces no legal effect whatsoever. Absolutely null from the beginning (e.g., contract to sell illegal substances or illegal object).

💡 Real-Life Scenario: Voidable vs. Void

Voidable: A 16-year-old student buys a high-end laptop. The contract is valid until the parents or minor petition court to annul it.

Void: Two individuals sign a written agreement to trade smuggled goods. The contract is completely void (inexistent) and unenforceable in court.

1. Article 1156 defines an obligation as a juridical necessity to:

Explanation: Art. 1156 explicitly defines an obligation as a juridical necessity to give, to do, or not to do.

2. Accidental overpayment received through GCash generates an obligation to return under which source?

Explanation: Solutio Indebiti is a quasi-contract created to prevent unjust enrichment when money or property is delivered by mistake.

3. What standard of care is required for an obligor to preserve a specific thing (Art. 1163)?

Explanation: Unless the law or contract states otherwise, Art. 1163 requires the diligence of a good father of a family.

4. Deliberate intent to evade the normal fulfillment of an obligation is known as:

Explanation: Fraud (Dolo) under Art. 1170 is deliberate evasion of an obligation's performance.

5. Which of the following is NOT an essential requisite of a contract under Article 1318?

Explanation: Contracts are generally consensual (Art. 1318). Notarization is not an essential element for validity, though required for certain public documents.

6. A contract entered into by an un-emancipated minor without parental consent is categorized as:

Explanation: Incapacity of one of the parties (such as minority) makes a contract voidable (valid until annulled by court).

7. A contract with an illegal object (e.g., selling prohibited drugs) is:

Explanation: Under Art. 1409, contracts whose cause or object is contrary to law or public order are void and inexistent from the beginning.

8. What is the legal effect of a Fortuitous Event (Art. 1174) when a debtor is NOT in delay?

Explanation: Under Art. 1174, a person is not liable for unforeseeable or inevitable events unless there is delay, a contrary law or stipulation, or the obligation requires assumption of risk.
